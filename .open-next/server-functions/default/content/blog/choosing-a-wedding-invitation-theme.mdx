Most theme advice is about taste, which is not much help when two families have four opinions. What follows is about the decisions that turn out to matter afterwards.

## Match the tradition before the aesthetic

The first filter is not "what do I like". It is "what does this wedding actually look like". A Nikah invitation and a Punjabi wedding invitation are not interchangeable with a colour swap — the motifs, the scripts and the greeting lines are different, and guests notice immediately when they are wrong.

Start by narrowing to the themes built for your tradition, then choose within that set. It cuts a paralysing decision down to three or four options, and it removes the risk of the mistake that actually embarrasses people.

## Match the formality of the function

The other useful filter is how formal the invitation should feel — and this varies by function, not by wedding.

- **The wedding itself** is the most formal thing you will send. This is where ornate, traditional and restrained all work.
- **Sangeet and mehndi** invitations can be much lighter. A playful theme that would be wrong for the wedding is right here.
- **The reception** sits somewhere between, and often goes to a wider list including colleagues, which pushes it back towards formal.

If you are sending one invitation covering everything, it should sit at the formality of the most formal function on it.

## Four tests worth running before you commit

<Steps>
<Step title="Look at it on a phone, at arm's length">
Not on your laptop. Almost every guest will open it on a phone, many outdoors, several with the brightness down. If the names are not immediately readable at arm's length, the theme is too delicate.
</Step>
<Step title="Screenshot it and send it to yourself">
Guests screenshot invitations constantly to forward them. Some themes survive that and some turn into a grey smear. Test it.
</Step>
<Step title="Put the longest name on it">
Compound surnames, both sets of parents, a long venue name. Ornate frames that look beautiful with "Ananya & Rohan" often break down with the full formal names.
</Step>
<Step title="Show it to someone from the older generation">
Not for approval — for legibility. If your grandmother has to hold the phone at three distances to read the date, the type is too small or the contrast too low, and she is exactly the guest you cannot afford to lose.
</Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Try more than one">
Because content and theme are stored separately, switching themes costs nothing and loses nothing. Build the invitation once, then look at it in three or four themes before deciding. Most people change their mind after seeing their own names in it, not before.
</Callout>

## What matters less than people think

**Matching the decor.** Guests receive the invitation months before they see the mandap, and almost never compare them. Spend the energy on legibility instead.

**The trend.** Invitations are looked at for a few weeks and then, occasionally, for decades. A theme chosen because it is current tends to date exactly as fast as the trend that produced it.

**Animation.** A nice opening is a nice opening. It is not what makes an invitation good, and on a poor connection it is the first thing to become an obstacle. If a theme's animation delays the names appearing, that is a defect, not a feature.

## What matters more than people think

**Contrast.** The single most common problem: gold text on cream looks exquisite on a designer's monitor and disappears on a phone in daylight.

**How the schedule reads.** Most guests come to the invitation for one piece of information — when and where. A theme that makes the schedule beautiful but hard to scan is working against the thing the invitation is for.

**The script your names are set in.** If any part of the invitation is in Hindi, Tamil, Gujarati or Urdu, the theme needs a typeface that renders that script properly. A theme that falls back to a system font for the Devanagari line will look visibly unfinished next to its English.

## A reasonable way to decide

Narrow by tradition. Narrow again by formality. Put your real names and your real venue into the two or three that remain. Look at all of them on a phone, one after another, and pick the one where you can read everything without trying.

That is usually the right answer, and it is rarely the one that looked best in the theme gallery.
