The choice is usually framed as tradition against convenience. That framing is not very useful, because the two formats are good at genuinely different things and most families need both.

Read that table honestly and the answer is not "digital wins". It is that the printed card is an object and the digital invitation is a service. They are not competing at the same thing.

## The printed card is a gesture, not a delivery mechanism

The reason a printed card still matters in an Indian wedding is that giving one is an act. You visit the house, you hand it over, you are given tea, you sit for twenty minutes. The card is the reason for the visit, not the content of it.

Nobody who receives a card that way is checking it for the venue's Maps pin. They already know where the hall is, or they will ask.

<Callout type="note" title="Where the printed card genuinely fails">
It fails at everything that happens after it is delivered. Venue changed? Reprint. Timings shifted? Reprint. Want to know how many are coming? Start calling. Cousin in Toronto? Courier, and hope.
</Callout>

## The digital invitation is the logistics layer

Everything the card cannot do, a link can, because a link points at something that is allowed to change.

- **It updates.** The entrance moves to Gate 3 the night before; you edit it and every guest sees the new one.
- **It counts.** RSVPs arrive as a list rather than as ninety WhatsApp replies you have to tally by hand.
- **It navigates.** One tap opens Maps. This matters more than anything else on the day itself.
- **It travels.** Relatives abroad get it the same evening, in the same form as everyone else.
- **It survives.** After the wedding it becomes where the photographs live.

## How most families actually split it

The rule that works, and the one nearly every family arrives at independently:

<Steps>
<Step title="Print for the people you will visit">
Elders, close family, the families you have a relationship with that involves showing up at their door. Usually a much smaller number than the full guest list — often fifty to a hundred cards rather than four hundred.
</Step>
<Step title="Send digitally to everyone else">
Colleagues, college friends, extended family, everyone abroad. This is the majority of the list and the group for whom a card is a courier problem rather than a gesture.
</Step>
<Step title="Put the link on the printed card too">
A QR code or a short URL on the card, so even the people who got the physical card have the schedule, the map and the RSVP form in their pocket. This is the step that makes both formats work together instead of competing.
</Step>
</Steps>

That third step is the one families skip and later wish they hadn't. The card in the drawer cannot tell anyone that the sangeet moved to 8 pm. The link on it can.

## On budget

The honest position: a digital invitation does not save money if you were going to print anyway, and most families are. What it changes is the *shape* of the cost. Print cost scales with your guest list and with every change; a digital invitation does not care whether you send it to eighty people or eight hundred, and it does not charge you for changing your mind.

Where families do save is in printing fewer cards, not none — going from four hundred to a hundred, and sending the rest as a link.

## The one thing that decides it

If the invitation needs to *do* something — collect responses, give directions, absorb a change, reach someone overseas — it needs to be digital, regardless of what else you print.

If it needs to *mean* something to someone who will keep it in a box, print it and take it to their house yourself.

Most weddings need both, which is why the argument about which is better has never quite resolved.
