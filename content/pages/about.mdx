## A box under the bed

My mother keeps a box under her bed. In it are wedding cards — her own, her sister's, cards from weddings in the family going back more than thirty years. Thick paper, gold foil, red envelopes, some of them with the rice still caught in the fold.

She has never thrown one away. Nobody in our family has.

That is the thing about an Indian wedding invitation. It is not a notification. It is the object that says a family thought of you, sat down, wrote your name, and came to your house to hand it over. The information on it — the date, the hall, the muhurat — stops being useful the day after the wedding. The card stays for decades.

I started thinking about this properly while helping with a cousin's wedding.

The venue changed eleven days before the ceremony. Four hundred cards had already gone out. What followed was a fortnight of phone calls and a WhatsApp message that some people saw and some did not — and on the day, a handful of guests still arrived at the original hall.

Meanwhile my aunt was counting RSVPs on the back of an envelope, relatives abroad were being sent photographs of the card, and my grandmother — who reads Hindi far more comfortably than English — was handed a card printed entirely in English.

</SplitFeature>

None of that is a failure of the card. It is a failure of asking a printed object to do things a printed object cannot do: change, count, travel, and speak more than one language.

## What we built

Amantrika is the other half of the card. The invitation as something that lives at a link: it updates when the venue moves, it collects the RSVPs into a list instead of a scroll, it opens in the language the person reading it is most comfortable in, it gives directions, and afterwards it holds the photographs.

It is not a replacement for the box under the bed. Most families we talk to print cards for the people they visit in person, and send a link to everyone else. That seems right to us. **The card is the gesture; the link is the logistics.**

<Section eyebrow="Our promises" title="What we will not do" lead="Some of these are unusual to state publicly. We would rather be held to them." tone="ornate">

<PromiseList>

<Promise title="Your invitation is yours">
We never publish anyone's invitation, photographs or guest details in a public gallery without being asked first — and asking means a checkbox you tick, not a line buried in terms nobody reads.
</Promise>

<Promise title="Guests are not tracked for our benefit">
Hosts can see how many people opened the invitation, because that is genuinely useful when you have sent it to four hundred people. Nobody outside your dashboard sees anything, and no advertising script ever loads on a guest's page.
</Promise>

<Promise title="No invented social proof">
If you do not see testimonials on this site yet, it is because we would rather show none than write fake ones.
</Promise>

<Promise title="The link never changes">
Whatever you shared keeps working — through edits, through payment, through the venue moving the week before.
</Promise>

<Promise title="No dark patterns at checkout">
No pre-ticked boxes, no countdown timers invented to rush you, no price that changes when you hesitate.
</Promise>

</PromiseList>
